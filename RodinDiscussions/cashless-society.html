The Transition to a Cashless Society

Discussed March 3, 2026

Here is a summary of the core talking points discussed in the meeting transcript regarding the transition to a cashless society:

  • The Global Shift Toward Cashless Transactions: The group noted that countries like China, Sweden, Norway, and Denmark are heavily cashless. They cited data predicting a 35% increase in digital wallet users by 2029, totaling 5.8 billion users globally.
  • The Impact on the US Dollar and Federal Reserve: A core question raised was how an entirely cashless society affects the global value of the US dollar. While some worried about a "shift away from the dollar," others countered that cashless transactions are still dollar-based, just processed digitally rather than via paper notes. They also questioned how the Federal Reserve would manage the money supply without physical cash, concluding that it would shift to managing electronic transaction paperwork and credit/debt balances.
  • Consumer Convenience vs. Hidden Merchant Fees: Participants shared personal experiences using digital wallets (like Apple Cash, Venmo, and Zelle) or tapping phones at restaurants (using services like Toast). However, a major complaint was raised regarding the "robbery" of credit card processing fees. Merchants are forced to pay an average of 2.65% plus 10 cents per transaction, costs that are ultimately passed down to consumers through marked-up prices. Average credit card interest rates of 21.5% were also heavily criticized as "usury".
  • The Problem of the Unbanked and Underprivileged: The group discussed how a 100% cashless system isolates lower-income populations, the homeless, and undocumented individuals who rely entirely on cash. In the US, approximately 4% of households (roughly 14 million people) are unbanked or underbanked. They mentioned potential solutions, such as cash-to-debit card kiosks or government-loaded benefit cards.
  • Security Risks, Cybersecurity, and Infrastructure Failures: Identity theft and system vulnerabilities were highlighted as massive downsides to an entirely digital economy, citing the Capital One data breach. To combat this, they noted that advanced cashless nations like Sweden and Norway are actually passing laws to protect cash access and urging citizens to keep physical cash at home as a safeguard against cyber warfare or infrastructure failure.
  • The Tracking and Surveillance Element: Digital transactions create a complete paper trail. This was viewed as an advantage for society and business owners—such as preventing employees from skimming cash—but a major disadvantage for workers wanting unreported cash tips/wages to avoid taxes. It was also jokingly noted that mobile transactions make it impossible to hide personal spending from a spouse.
  • Generational Resistance and Demographics: The speakers acknowledged that their own demographic (Baby Boomers) tends to resist digital wallets and prefer cash options. However, they concluded that fully cashless adoption is an inevitable evolution over the next 10 to 20 years because younger generations (Gen Z and those under 40) are completely comfortable without cash.
  • Legacy Systems in Government: Near the end of the meeting, the group briefly discussed the irony of modern financial tech operating alongside outdated government systems, noting that the IRS and several major corporations still rely heavily on old COBOL code.
