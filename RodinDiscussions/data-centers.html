Data Centers, AI, and Local Communities

The discussion focused on the rapid expansion of AI data centers, their enormous demands for electricity and cooling, their economic benefits and costs, and the decisions local governments must make when considering projects such as Project Blue in the Tucson area.

1. The rapid expansion of data centers

  • The group discussed the extraordinary scale of investment in data centers to support artificial intelligence.
  • One participant cited approximately 5,500 data centers in the United States, with thousands more planned or under construction, and estimated that major technology companies would spend more than $600 billion on construction in 2026.
  • Participants questioned whether the pace of expansion is sustainable and whether the economic benefits justify the resources being consumed.
  • There was broad agreement that data centers are becoming essential to modern computing and that their expansion is unlikely to stop.

2. The benefits and necessity of artificial intelligence

  • Participants asked whether all this computing capacity is genuinely necessary.
  • Supporters argued that AI is becoming integral to American industry, business operations, scientific research, healthcare, and productivity. They contended that many major industries would struggle to function without it.
  • Others questioned how much computing capacity is being devoted to less productive uses, such as AI companions, virtual girlfriends, and social entertainment.
  • The group disagreed over whether these recreational applications account for a substantial share of data-center demand. No reliable statistics were presented to settle the question.
  • A related concern was whether the United States must continue expanding AI infrastructure to remain competitive internationally, particularly with China.

3. The economic consequences of massive AI investment

  • Participants compared the recent investment in AI with the historic scale of earlier technological and industrial projects.
  • One participant cited a comparison suggesting that a single large data center could cost many times more than the entire Manhattan Project.
  • The group discussed whether the enormous investment in AI is creating sustainable economic value or contributing to a possible investment bubble.
  • Some participants questioned the profitability of companies developing AI systems, citing a reported projection that OpenAI could lose $100 billion through 2030. The figure and its basis were not independently verified during the discussion.
  • Others explained that technology companies sometimes offer products cheaply or for free to build adoption and market share, expecting to earn money later from commercial services.
  • One investment strategy proposed was to invest in established businesses that use AI to develop profitable products rather than in AI developers that must first recover enormous capital expenditures.

4. Do data centers benefit the communities that host them?

  • This was one of the central themes of the discussion.
  • Data centers generate substantial construction activity, but the group emphasized that permanent employment is generally limited once a facility is operational. Staffing may consist largely of technicians, maintenance personnel, and security.
  • Participants questioned whether municipalities receive sufficient tax revenue and other economic benefits to compensate for the demands placed on local infrastructure.
  • Possible community benefits include property taxes, construction-related spending, hotel stays, restaurants, and other local economic activity.
  • However, the group worried that these benefits might be overstated, particularly when temporary construction employment is presented as a lasting source of jobs.

5. Tax incentives and the responsibilities of local government

  • Participants criticized municipalities for granting large tax breaks to attract data centers, particularly when the companies involved are highly profitable or have substantial financial resources.
  • They argued that elected officials may focus on short-term job creation and political benefits rather than long-term community costs.
  • Several participants maintained that companies should pay for the electricity, water, infrastructure, and other resources their facilities require rather than shifting those costs onto residents.
  • A central conclusion was that communities should negotiate contracts that protect local taxpayers and ratepayers, even if that means losing a proposed project.
  • One participant argued that a poorly negotiated data-center deal could leave a community worse off than having no data center at all.

6. Project Blue and the Tucson-area debate

  • The discussion returned repeatedly to Project Blue and the Tucson City Council's decision to reject it.
  • Participants wondered whether the city could have negotiated a better agreement rather than treating the decision as a choice between accepting the project as proposed and rejecting it entirely.
  • Some participants had heard that city and county officials had conducted due diligence, but the details were unclear. A nondisclosure agreement reportedly limited what could be made public.
  • The group suggested that Tucson should clearly explain the economic reasons for its decision, if the project was rejected because the terms were unfavorable.
  • A public explanation could help other municipalities evaluate similar proposals and learn from Tucson's experience.
  • Participants also raised the possibility of federal intervention to override local zoning decisions, expressing concern about communities losing control over what is built near their homes.

7. Electricity demand and rising utility costs

  • Data centers require enormous amounts of electricity to operate their processors and cooling systems.
  • Participants warned that utilities may need to build new generating capacity, substations, transmission lines, and other infrastructure to serve them.
  • These investments can increase electricity rates if utility companies spread the costs across all customers rather than charging the data centers that create the demand.
  • The group cited a claim from a Bloomberg report that electricity rates had risen by more than 260% over five years in areas with data centers. The claim was mentioned in the conversation but not independently verified.
  • Participants agreed that contracts should ensure data-center operators pay an appropriate share of the infrastructure costs they cause.

8. Water consumption, cooling, and noise

  • Cooling is a major operational challenge because densely packed computer servers generate large quantities of heat.
  • The group discussed liquid cooling and chilled-water systems, which can remove heat effectively but introduce plumbing risks around expensive electronic equipment.
  • Traditional data centers also use arrangements that separate hot exhaust air from cooler intake air.
  • Participants raised concerns about the large quantities of water some cooling systems require, particularly in Arizona's dry climate.
  • Noise was another concern. The group referred to the persistent humming associated with data centers in Northern Virginia.
  • Improved cooling technologies were viewed as important, although the participants recognized that these facilities would continue to impose environmental and infrastructure demands.

9. The physical design and operating requirements of data centers

  • One participant described experience building a data center of approximately 30,000 square feet.
  • The discussion identified three critical requirements:
    • Clean, uninterrupted power: Computer systems cannot tolerate power interruptions or fluctuations, requiring specialized power-conditioning equipment, batteries, or motor-generator systems.
    • Cooling: Both air-cooled and water-cooled systems have advantages and disadvantages, including the risk of water leaks near electronics.
    • Fire suppression: Expensive computing equipment requires specialized fire-protection systems. The discussion included Halon, an older fire-suppression agent that can be dangerous to people in enclosed spaces.
  • Raised floors, sometimes 18 inches to three feet high, provide space for cabling and air circulation in traditional data-center designs.
  • Although construction and installation require many workers, participants stressed that a completed facility can operate with relatively few employees.

10. Data-center size, location, and computing efficiency

  • The group explored whether one enormous data center would be more efficient than numerous smaller facilities.
  • The technical explanation was that concentrating processors can improve communication efficiency because computing components must exchange information rapidly.
  • However, distance also matters: facilities serving users far away introduce network latency.
  • Splitting a large computing installation into several locations can create additional communication delays between the sites.
  • Participants concluded that neither a single worldwide facility nor a large number of smaller centers is automatically best. The appropriate size and location depend on computing requirements, proximity to users, and the need for real-time responses.

11. How long do data centers remain useful?

  • Participants asked how long a data center remains technologically relevant as processors become faster and more capable.
  • One participant noted that NVIDIA has introduced new generations of GPUs on roughly two-year cycles.
  • The discussion distinguished replacing computing equipment from rebuilding the entire facility. A data center's physical structure may remain usable even as its processors are upgraded.
  • Participants also noted that engineers are working to reduce power consumption and heat generation in newer equipment.
  • This raised a broader economic question: whether the massive initial investment can be recovered before successive equipment upgrades require further spending.

12. Solar power and its limitations

  • Solar energy was initially suggested as a natural solution for powering data centers, particularly in sunny Arizona.
  • One participant estimated that solar panels covering the roof of a medium-sized, approximately 100,000-square-foot data center would generate less than 2% of its electricity needs.
  • Based on that participant's calculation, approximately 250 acres of solar panels would be needed to power one medium-sized facility.
  • The group noted that cooling alone can consume a substantial portion of a data center's energy.
  • The principal takeaway was that rooftop solar would be insufficient to meet the electricity requirements of a large data center, even in a sunny location. The acreage and energy estimates were discussed as calculations by participants, not independently verified figures.

13. Nuclear power and small modular reactors

  • Nuclear power emerged as a possible way to provide reliable, large-scale electricity without depending entirely on the public grid.
  • Participants discussed small modular reactors, including Bill Gates's investment in advanced nuclear technology.
  • A participant cited a possible reactor output in the hundreds of megawatts and suggested that a suitably sized reactor could potentially support a data center's electricity and cooling requirements.
  • The group also noted constraints: reactor construction, specialized equipment, turbine availability, cost, safety, and lengthy development timelines.
  • A significant obstacle is finding a location where the public will accept a nuclear facility.
  • Some participants argued that data centers should provide their own dedicated power generation rather than transferring their electricity demands to local residents.

14. Alternative locations and energy sources

  • The group briefly explored underground data centers, including facilities built in retired limestone mines or caves, where surrounding rock might help moderate temperatures.
  • Geothermal energy was discussed, with Iceland cited as a potential example because of its geothermal resources.
  • The participants also mentioned nuclear fusion as a longer-term possibility, with AI potentially helping accelerate scientific progress.
  • These alternatives were exploratory ideas rather than established solutions endorsed by the group.

15. Could data centers be located in space?

  • One participant suggested placing data centers in space, where solar energy is continuously available in some orbital configurations.
  • Elon Musk's plans for large satellite constellations prompted discussion of the potential for space-based computing.
  • Participants questioned whether communication delays would make space-based facilities impractical for interactive AI applications.
  • The discussion distinguished two kinds of computing:
    • Predictive modeling: Processing data to forecast outcomes, where the result may not be needed immediately.
    • Interactive generative AI: Responding to a person or system in real time, where even modest delays can impair usability.
  • Space-based computing might be more suitable for some non-real-time workloads than for conversational AI.
  • Participants also raised concerns about space debris, satellite failures, and the environmental consequences of deploying enormous numbers of satellites.

16. International competition and the concentration of data centers in the United States

  • Participants compared data-center counts across countries. Figures cited during the meeting were approximately 5,427 in the United States, 529 in Germany, and 440 in China.
  • The group observed that the United States appeared to dominate the figures presented.
  • They debated whether this reflected America's business-driven investment model, differences in government involvement, or the scale of American demand for AI.
  • Participants questioned whether China was genuinely ahead in AI and whether competition with China justified the current pace of American investment.
  • No definitive conclusion was reached about the relative efficiency or technological superiority of the countries discussed.

17. Competition for workers and the impact on housing and construction

  • The construction boom may divert skilled labor and materials from other projects, including hospitals, schools, apartments, and affordable housing.
  • Participants anticipated that concentrated demand for skilled construction workers could raise labor costs and delay other developments.
  • They also discussed the possibility of higher prices for cement, lumber, and other building materials.
  • These effects could compound existing housing affordability problems, particularly for younger people trying to purchase homes.
  • The concern extended beyond data-center operating costs to the wider economic consequences of concentrating so much construction activity in one industry.

18. Property taxes and the true value of the local economic benefit

  • Participants discussed whether property taxes alone provide a sufficient return to communities hosting data centers.
  • One concern was that operators might appeal property valuations, arguing that highly specialized buildings have limited alternative uses.
  • Others pointed out that construction workers and visiting personnel can generate additional local spending and tax revenue.
  • Figures of up to $27 billion in taxes nationally and $2.6 billion for Arizona were mentioned, but the underlying estimates and assumptions were not established during the discussion.
  • The group emphasized the need to evaluate actual net revenue after accounting for infrastructure costs, tax incentives, utility expenses, and other public obligations.

19. The need for a broader public policy

  • The group debated whether individual companies pursuing profits could be expected to make decisions that maximize the public good.
  • One proposal was a coordinated national effort that would encourage broader sharing of technological benefits, although participants acknowledged the difficulties posed by proprietary information and commercial competition.
  • Another approach was to let municipalities learn from one another by publishing the economic reasons for accepting or rejecting proposed projects.
  • Participants also raised the possibility of national rules governing data-center development, while questioning who would write those rules and how much authority the federal government should have over local decisions.

20. The possibility of an AI investment bubble

  • The discussion returned to whether the enormous capital expenditures associated with AI might represent a speculative bubble similar to the technology boom of roughly 25 years ago.
  • Some participants were skeptical of investing in AI developers that are spending heavily without demonstrating near-term profitability.
  • Others argued that large upfront losses are not necessarily evidence of a failed business model because technology companies may spend years building infrastructure, attracting customers, and developing markets.
  • The group distinguished the financial prospects of AI infrastructure companies from those of established businesses that use AI to improve productivity.
  • No consensus was reached on whether the current investment boom constitutes a bubble.

Overall conclusions

The discussion produced several recurring themes:

1. Data centers are becoming essential infrastructure for AI and modern business, but

their expansion has substantial electricity, cooling, construction, and environmental consequences.

2. The economic case for hosting a data center must be evaluated locally. Construction

jobs and projected tax revenue do not automatically translate into lasting community benefits.

3. Municipalities should negotiate firm, transparent agreements that protect residents

from higher utility bills, infrastructure costs, water demands, and other externalities.

4. Power generation and cooling are major constraints. Solar, nuclear power, small

modular reactors, underground facilities, and improved cooling technologies all warrant consideration, but each has limitations.

5. The optimal design is not necessarily the largest possible data center. Computing

needs, latency, location, power availability, and operating costs all influence the appropriate scale.

6. The effects extend beyond AI itself. Competition for construction labor and materials

may worsen housing affordability and delay other public and private projects.

7. The central unresolved question is how to balance national technological

competitiveness with local economic interests. The group generally accepted that data centers will continue to be built, but stressed that communities should not accept unfavorable deals simply out of fear of losing investment.
